## Find dry fuel
- **Dead branches still on the tree** stay drier than wood on the ground. Snap them: a crisp snap means dry inside.
- Look under overhangs, dense conifers, logs or fallen trees.
- Split wood open. The **inside is dry** even when the outside is wet.
- **Fatwood** (resin-rich pine stump or branch base) and **birch bark** burn even when wet. Look for dead standing wood and dry bark.

## Make dry tinder
- **Feather sticks:** shave thin curls on a dry stick, leaving them attached. Fuzzy curls catch easily.
- Pocket lint, cotton balls with petroleum jelly, dryer lint, and fire starters work well.
- Dry grass, bark and leaves from a protected spot, or inside your clothing.
- Keep tinder in a bag or in your jacket until you need it.

## Build the fire
1. Choose a spot out of the rain: under a tree with a canopy, a rock overhang or beneath a tarp.
2. **Make a platform** of flat stones, green logs or bark so the fire isn't on wet ground.
3. Use a small amount of tinder and have lots of kindling, from pencil-thin to thumb-thick, ready before you strike.
4. Build a **teepee** or lean-to against a log. Start thin and build up slowly.
5. Light it, shelter it with your body or a windbreak, and add pencil-thin sticks as it catches.

## Keep it going
- Stack wet wood next to the fire to dry before you add it.
- Feed small sticks. Don't smother it with big pieces.
- Reflect heat with a wall of logs or stones behind the fire.
- Keep spare dry wood under cover for later.

> Rain extinguishes a weak fire fast. Do not start until you have enough fuel ready, from tinder to wrist-thick pieces.
