Amateur ("ham") radio has been the backup when phones, internet and power failed. Volunteer operators run emergency nets and relay messages to officials and hospitals.

## Licenses
Transmitting on ham bands normally needs a license, which you get by passing a short exam. Licensed hams use call signs. Anyone can **listen** with no license.

In a **real, life-threatening emergency** with no other way to call for help, using any radio you have is widely accepted and almost never questioned. Learning and getting licensed before then is far better.

## The basics
- **VHF 2 meters (144 to 148 MHz):** the most common handheld band.
- **UHF 70 centimeters (420 to 450 MHz):** also common on handhelds.
- **Simplex:** radio to radio, no repeater. Range is a few km on a handheld. **146.520 MHz** is the national 2 meter calling frequency. **446.000 MHz** is the 70 cm calling frequency.
- **Repeaters:** a tall antenna that re-transmits your signal, covering a town or region. You need its frequency, the offset and a tone, which local ham clubs publish.
- **Nets:** scheduled group calls. During disasters, emergency nets run on local repeaters.

## Finding people
1. Tune to 146.520 MHz (simplex) and listen. Then tune local repeaters.
2. Listen first. If someone is already talking, wait.
3. Say your call sign or name, say you are monitoring, and ask if anyone is on frequency.
4. In a real emergency, say **"Emergency traffic"** or **"Mayday"** and give your message. See [Making an Emergency Radio Call](radio-calls.md).

## Etiquette in a disaster
- Keep calls short. Leave the channel free for emergency traffic.
- Use plain language, not jargon, and say each message once clearly.
- Write messages down and repeat them back.
- Follow the instructions of the net control station.

## Gear
- A handheld (HT) with a spare battery and a better antenna than the stock "rubber duck".
- A printed list of local repeaters and frequencies.
- A way to charge: a car charger, power bank or small solar panel.
