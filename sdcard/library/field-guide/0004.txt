LENS
A magnifying glass, eyeglasses for far-sightedness, or a clear bottle of water can focus sunlight onto dark tinder. Needs bright sun.

BOW DRILL (LAST RESORT)
1. Fireboard: dry, soft, dead wood (willow, cedar, poplar, basswood) about 2 cm thick.
2. Spindle: straight, dry stick of the same wood, thumb-thick, about 20 cm long.
3. Bearing block: a hard piece of wood or stone with a dent, to press down on the spindle.
4. Bow: a curved stick about arm's length with cord tied loosely so you can twist the spindle into it.
5. Cut a notch from the edge of the fireboard to a small burned-in hole. Spin the spindle fast with long, steady bow strokes while pressing down.
6. When smoking dust fills the notch and keeps smoking on its own, you have an ember. Tip it into a tinder bundle and blow gently until it flames.

WARNING: Friction fire takes practice and a lot of energy. Learn it before you need it.

FIRE SAFETY

CHOOSING A SITE
- Clear a 3 m (10 ft) circle down to bare soil. Remove leaves and dry grass.
- Stay away from overhanging branches, tree roots, peat and dry grass.
- Do not build against a large rock you want to sit by; wet rocks from rivers can crack or burst when heated.
- Pay attention to wind. Sparks carry far in dry weather.

CARBON MONOXIDE
WARNING: Never burn a fire, stove, charcoal or generator inside a closed tent, car, snow cave or small room. Carbon monoxide has no smell and kills in your sleep.

Signs of poisoning: headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, sleepiness. Get the person into fresh air at once.

PUTTING A FIRE OUT
1. Let it burn down to ash if you can.
2. Pour water on it and stir the ashes. Pour more and stir again.
3. If water is scarce, smother with soil or sand and stir, but do not just bury it; buried coals can stay hot for a day.
4. Feel for heat with the back of your hand held close. If it is too hot to touch, it is too hot to leave.

IF A WILDFIRE THREATENS
- Move to areas with little fuel: rock, gravel, a recently burned area, or across a wide river.
- Move downhill and across the slope, not uphill. Fire races uphill.
- Cover your skin with wool or cotton, not synthetics, and breathe through a damp cloth.

COOKING OVER A FIRE

COOK ON COALS, NOT FLAMES
Flames burn the outside and leave the inside raw. Let a fire burn down to a bed of glowing coals, or rake coals to one side of the fire to cook on while the other side keeps burning.

Hand test for heat: hold your palm about 10 cm (4 in) above the coals.
- 2 seconds before it is too hot: very hot
- 4-5 seconds: medium, good for most cooking
- 6-8 seconds: low, good for simmering

METHODS
- Boiling: the safest way to cook anything you are unsure about, and it makes water safe. Hang the pot from a tripod or support it on rocks or green logs.
- Foil packets: wrap food in two layers of foil and set it on coals. Turn once. Vegetables take 15-20 minutes, fish about 10.
- Skewers and spits: use green (live) wood, so it does not burn through. Avoid poisonous woods like yew and oleander.
- Hot stone: heat a flat, dry stone at the edge of the fire and cook on it like a pan.
- Pit cooking: heat stones in a fire in a pit, rake out the fire, wrap food in green leaves, cover with soil and leave for hours.

WARNING: Never heat rocks from rivers, lakes or wet ground. Trapped water can make them crack or explode.

COOK MEAT FULLY
Wild meat and fish can carry parasites. Cook until juices run clear and there is no pink.
- Poultry and birds: 74 C (165 F) inside
- Ground or chopped meat: 71 C (160 F)
- Whole cuts of game: 71 C (160 F) is safest for wild meat
- Fish: until it flakes easily and looks opaque

SAFETY
- Keep a pot of water or a pile of soil next to the cooking fire.
- Use a stick or cloth to handle hot pots; metal handles get very hot.
- Wash hands before handling food, and keep raw meat away from cooked food.

=== SHELTER ===

SHELTER BASICS AND SITE CHOICE

In cold, wet or windy weather, shelter is your top priority after first aid. A good shelter is small, dry, out of the wind and insulated from the ground.

HOW YOU LOSE HEAT
- Conduction: touching cold ground or rock. The ground steals heat fastest, so insulate under you first.
- Convection: wind blowing heat away.
- Evaporation: wet clothes and sweat.
- Radiation: heat leaving your body to the open sky.

CHOOSING A SITE
- Flat, slightly raised and well-drained ground.
- Near building materials, firewood and water, but at least 60 m (200 ft) from streams.
- Out of the wind: behind rocks, thick trees or a ridge.

AVOID
- Dead trees and hanging branches ("widowmakers") above you.
- Dry stream beds and valley floors (floods, and cold air sinks there at night).
- Bases of cliffs (rockfall) and avalanche slopes.
- Insect nests and animal trails.

WARNING: Pile at least 10-20 cm (4-8 in) of dry leaves, grass, pine boughs or a pack between you and the ground, even inside a tent. It compresses when you lie on it.

BEFORE DARK
Start your shelter at least two hours before sunset. Building in the dark is slow and dangerous.

LEAN-TO SHELTER

A lean-to is quick to build and pairs well with a fire in front. It blocks wind and rain from one side.

[Picture: Lean-to with fire reflector]

BUILDING IT
1. Find two trees about 2-3 m apart, or drive two forked sticks into the ground.
2. Tie or wedge a strong ridge pole across them at about chest height.
3. Lean long poles against the ridge pole on the windward side, close together, at about 45-60 degrees.
4. Weave smaller branches across the poles.
5. Cover with layers of bark, leafy branches, grass or a tarp, starting at the bottom so each layer overlaps the one below, like shingles.
6. Make a thick bed of dry insulation inside.

ADDING A FIRE REFLECTOR
Build a fire about one step in front of the open side. Behind the fire, stack green logs between stakes to make a wall. It throws heat back into the shelter.

WARNING: The back of the lean-to must face the wind. Check the wind direction before you start, and watch for changes.

DEBRIS HUT

The debris hut keeps you warm with no fire, sleeping bag or tools. It works like a giant sleeping bag made of leaves.

[Picture: Debris hut cross-section]

BUILDING IT
1. Find a ridge pole a bit longer than your height.
2. Prop one end on a stump, rock or a pair of crossed sticks about waist high. The other end rests on the ground.
3. Lie down under it to check: it should be just big enough to fit you. Bigger means colder.
4. Lean sticks along both sides of the pole like ribs, close together.
5. Lay a lattice of small branches over the ribs so leaves do not fall through.
6. Pile on dry leaves, grass, pine needles and debris until the walls are 60-90 cm (2-3 ft) thick. Thicker for colder nights.
7. Add some branches on top to keep the wind from blowing the leaves off.
8. Stuff the inside with dry leaves for a thick bed, and keep a pile by the entrance.
9. Crawl in feet first and pull the leaf pile in to plug the door.

WARNING: It takes a lot of debris, often a few hours of work. Start early and gather more than you think you need.

TARP AND PONCHO SHELTERS

A tarp is the fastest shelter there is. Even a large trash bag or emergency blanket can become a roof.

[Picture: Tarp A-frame]

A-FRAME
1. Tie a cord tight between two trees at waist to chest height.
2. Hang the tarp over the cord so both sides are equal.
3. Stake or weight the corners down. Pull them out until the tarp is tight.
4. In wind, set the windward side lower or right to the ground.

LEAN-TO TARP
Tie the top edge of the tarp along the ridgeline and stake the bottom edge to the ground on the windward side. Good with a fire in front.

TIPS
- No grommets? Wrap a small stone in the tarp corner and tie the cord around it.
- Angle the roof steeply so rain runs off.
- Dig a shallow trench uphill of the shelter to steer water around it.
- Tie cord with a knot you can untie: a taut-line hitch or truckers hitch lets you tighten the line.

WARNING: An emergency blanket reflects heat back to you, but it holds condensation. Wrap it outside your insulation, not against your skin, when you can.

COLD WEATHER AND SNOW SHELTERS

STAY WARM WITH COLD
- C - Clean: dirty clothes lose insulation.
- O - avoid Overheating: remove layers before you sweat.
- L - Loose layers: tight clothing cuts circulation.
- D - Dry: wet clothing pulls heat away fast. Dry socks matter most.

SNOW TRENCH
1. Dig a trench as long as your body, about 1 m (3 ft) deep, in firm snow.
2. Roof it with branches, skis or a tarp, then pile snow on top.
3. Line the floor with thick insulation: boughs, packs, foam.

QUINZEE (SNOW MOUND)
1. Pile snow into a mound about 2 m (7 ft) high. Mix layers as you pile.
2. Let it settle for 1-2 hours.
3. Push sticks about 30 cm (1 ft) into the mound all over, to show wall thickness.
4. Dig a low entrance and hollow it out until you hit the sticks.
5. Poke a fist-sized air hole in the roof.
