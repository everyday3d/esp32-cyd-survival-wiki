1. Find the Big Dipper, a ladle shape of seven bright stars.
2. The two stars on the outer edge of the bowl are the pointers.
3. Follow the line from the bottom pointer through the top pointer, about 5 times the gap between them.
4. You reach Polaris, a moderately bright star on its own. It is the end of the handle of the Little Dipper.
5. Polaris is almost exactly over the North Pole. Facing it, you face north.

If the Big Dipper is low or hidden, look for Cassiopeia, a W shape on the opposite side of Polaris.

SOUTHERN HEMISPHERE: THE SOUTHERN CROSS
[Picture: Southern Cross]

1. Find the Southern Cross, four bright stars in a kite shape. Two bright pointer stars sit beside it.
2. Extend the long axis of the cross about 4.5 times its length from the foot.
3. That point is roughly the south pole of the sky. Drop straight down to the horizon: that is south.

ORION (BOTH HEMISPHERES)
Orion's Belt, three bright stars in a row, rises almost exactly due east and sets almost due west.

ANY STAR
Watch a star over two fixed sticks for 15 minutes. In the Northern Hemisphere: rising means you face east, sinking means west, moving right means south, moving left means north. Reverse north and south in the Southern Hemisphere.

MAP AND COMPASS BASICS

ORIENT THE MAP
1. Lay the compass on the map with its edge along a north-south grid line.
2. Turn the map and compass together until the red needle points to north on the map (allowing for declination, below).
3. The map now matches the land around you.

TAKE A BEARING TO A LANDMARK
1. Point the travel arrow on the compass at the landmark.
2. Turn the dial until the orienting arrow lines up under the red needle ("red in the shed").
3. Read the bearing at the index line.

FOLLOW A BEARING
1. Hold the compass flat in front of you, travel arrow forward.
2. Turn your body until the needle sits in the orienting arrow.
3. Pick a tree or rock on that line, walk to it, then repeat.

DECLINATION
Compass north (magnetic north) differs from map north. The difference, printed on most maps, can be 10-20 degrees or more in some regions. Adjust for it, or you will drift far off course over distance.

OTHER TIPS
- Keep metal, phones and magnets away from the compass.
- Aiming off: if heading to a point on a river or road, aim deliberately to one side, so you know which way to turn when you reach it.
- Handrails: follow long features like rivers, ridges or trails.
- Count paces to estimate distance: most people take about 60-70 double paces per 100 m on flat ground.

TRAVEL AND STAYING FOUND

BEFORE YOU GO
Tell someone your route and when to expect you. Carry a map of the area.

MARKING YOUR TRAIL
- Break branches at eye level, pointing your direction.
- Stack small rock piles (cairns) or make arrows with sticks.
- Leave notes at junctions: time, date, direction and condition.

IF YOU ARE LOST AND DECIDE TO MOVE
- Pick one direction and stick to it. People who wander in circles get more lost.
- Go downhill and follow water: streams lead to rivers, and rivers to roads and towns. But avoid steep canyons and waterfalls.
- Climb to a high point (safely) to look for roads, buildings, smoke or power lines.
- Follow power lines, fences, pipelines or railways. They lead somewhere.
- Travel in daylight. Stop and make camp well before dark.

WARNING: In fog, darkness or a whiteout, stop and wait. Walking blind leads to falls and getting more lost.

SIGNS OF PEOPLE
Roads, trails, fences, cut stumps, tire tracks, livestock, and the sound of traffic, dogs or machines.

NATURAL DIRECTION CLUES AND DAYLIGHT

HOW MUCH DAYLIGHT IS LEFT?
[Picture: Estimating time until sunset with your hand]

1. Stretch your arm out fully, palm toward you, fingers together and level.
2. Put your bottom finger on the horizon.
3. Count how many finger-widths fit between the horizon and the sun.
4. Each finger is roughly 15 minutes of daylight; four fingers is about an hour.

This is a rough guide that changes with your location and the season. Leave plenty of extra time. After sunset, twilight gives about 20-30 more minutes of usable light in most places.

SUN BASICS
- The sun rises roughly in the east and sets roughly in the west.
- North of the tropics, the midday sun is in the south. South of the tropics, it is in the north.
- Sunrise and sunset are only exactly east and west near the equinoxes (March and September). In summer they shift toward the pole; in winter, toward the equator.

CLUES THAT ARE OFTEN RIGHT (NORTHERN HEMISPHERE)
Reverse north and south in the Southern Hemisphere.
- Snow melts first on south-facing slopes and lasts longer on north-facing ones.
- South-facing slopes are often drier, with more open, sun-loving plants. North-facing slopes are often damper with denser growth.
- Prevailing winds shape trees and snowdrifts. If you know the usual wind direction in your area, bent trees and drift shapes point the way.
- Churches: older churches in Europe often have the altar at the east end.
- Satellite dishes in North America and Europe tend to face southward.

MYTHS - DO NOT RELY ON THEM
- "Moss grows on the north side of trees." Moss grows wherever it is damp and shady, which can be any side.
- "Ant hills are on the south side." Not reliable.
- Tree rings wider on one side depend on slope, light and wind, not direction.

WARNING: Combine several clues and confirm with the Shadow-Stick Method or the stars before committing to a direction.

=== SIGNALS & RESCUE ===

GROUND-TO-AIR SIGNALS

Large symbols on the ground can be seen from aircraft kilometers away.

[Picture: Ground-to-air signals]

THE KEY SYMBOLS
- V - Need assistance
- X - Need medical assistance
- N - No
- Y - Yes
- Arrow - Going in this direction
- SOS - Distress (understood everywhere)

MAKING THEM VISIBLE
- Make each symbol at least 3 m (10 ft) long, with lines about 1 m (3 ft) wide. Bigger is better.
- Use contrast: dark logs or rocks on snow, light stones or clothing on dark ground, trench lines in snow or sand so shadows show.
- Make straight lines and sharp angles. Nature has few of them, so they stand out.
- Pick an open area: a clearing, beach, ridge or frozen lake.

ANSWERING AIRCRAFT WITH YOUR BODY
- Both arms raised in a Y: Yes, we need help.
- One arm up, one arm down (diagonal N): No, we do not need help.

WARNING: If an aircraft rocks its wings or flashes lights, it has seen you.

SIGNAL MIRROR

A mirror flash can be seen up to 15 km (10 miles) or more on a sunny day. It is one of the best rescue signals.

[Picture: Aiming a signal mirror]

AIMING WITHOUT A SIGHTING HOLE
1. Hold the mirror just under your eye, shiny side out.
2. Stretch out your other arm and make a V with two fingers. Frame the target (aircraft, boat, hilltop) inside the V.
3. Tilt the mirror until the spot of sunlight lands on your fingers.
4. Keep the light in the V and rock the mirror slightly so it sweeps across the target.

MIRRORS WITH A SIGHTING HOLE
Look through the hole at the target. You will see a bright aiming spot; tilt the mirror until the spot sits on the target.

TIPS
- Anything shiny works: a phone screen, CD, polished can lid, foil.
- Sweep the horizon often, even when you see nothing. Pilots may see you before you see them.
- Do not keep flashing a pilot who has clearly seen you; it can blind them. Switch to ground signals.

SOS, WHISTLE, FIRE AND SMOKE

THE RULE OF THREE
Three of anything means distress: three fires in a triangle, three whistle blasts, three gunshots, three flashes.

SOS IN MORSE CODE
[Picture: SOS in Morse code]

Three short, three long, three short: ... --- .... Pause, then repeat. Use a flashlight, mirror, whistle or car horn.

WHISTLE
A whistle carries much farther than shouting and uses less energy. Blow three long blasts, then pause and listen. Repeat regularly. The answer is often two blasts.

SIGNAL FIRES
- Build three fires in a triangle, about 30 m (100 ft) apart, in an open area.
- Keep them ready to light: tinder and kindling under cover, with plenty of fuel nearby.
- Day: add green leaves, moss or wet wood to make thick white smoke, which shows against dark forest. Rubber or oil makes black smoke, which shows against snow or sand.
- Night: bright flames show best.

WARNING: Only light a signal fire when you hear or see a search aircraft or party, unless you have endless fuel. Never risk starting a wildfire.

OTHER SIGNALS
- Bright clothing or a space blanket spread out in the open.
- Flashlight or headlamp at night, in SOS pattern.
- Move: motion is easier to spot than a still person.
