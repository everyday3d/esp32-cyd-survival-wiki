SNOW BLINDNESS
Sunburn of the eye from sun reflecting off snow, water or sand. Painful, gritty, red, watery eyes, often hours later.
- Rest the eyes in the dark, with cool cloths over them.
- It usually heals in 1-2 days.
- Prevent it: wear sunglasses. If you have none, make slit goggles from bark, cardboard or cloth with thin horizontal slits, and darken the skin under your eyes with charcoal.

=== FOOD ===

FOOD IN A SURVIVAL SITUATION

Food is the last of the big survival needs. Most healthy people can go weeks without it. Hunger is unpleasant, but it is rarely what kills.

WHY FOOD STILL MATTERS
- It keeps you warm, especially at night in cold weather.
- It keeps your mind sharp and your mood up.
- Long-term survival, past a few days, needs a food plan.

RULES OF THUMB
- Do not spend more energy finding food than it gives you. Chasing animals is usually a losing trade.
- Eat high-energy foods you carried first, in small regular amounts.
- If water is very short, eat little. Digestion, especially of protein, uses water.
- Easy calories: insects, fish, shellfish from clean water, nuts, and plants you know with certainty.
- Cook all wild meat, fish and shellfish thoroughly.

BEST RETURNS FOR LITTLE EFFORT
1. Fishing with a line, hooks, or simple traps in streams
2. Insects and grubs
3. Familiar plants, nuts and berries you can identify with certainty
4. Trapping small animals (where it is legal and you know how)

See also: Foraging Plants Safely

FORAGING PLANTS SAFELY

WARNING: Only eat a plant if you can identify it with 100% certainty. Several deadly plants look like common edible ones.

NEVER EAT
- Wild mushrooms, unless you are an expert. Some deadly species look harmless, and symptoms can take a day to appear, too late to treat.
- Plants that look like wild carrot, parsnip or parsley (white umbrella-shaped flower clusters). This family includes water hemlock and poison hemlock, among the deadliest plants anywhere.
- Plants with milky or discolored sap (some exceptions exist, but avoid unless you know them).
- Beans, bulbs or seeds inside pods, unless known.
- Plants with an almond smell (can mean cyanide).
- White and yellow berries (most are poisonous); about half of red berries are too.

COMMONLY KNOWN SAFE FOODS (WHERE YOU KNOW THEM)
- Cattail: young shoots, roots and pollen. Found in marshes.
- Dandelion: leaves, flowers and roots.
- Clover: leaves and flowers, best cooked.
- Acorns: must be shelled, crushed and soaked in several changes of water to leach out bitter tannins.
- Pine: inner bark and needles (tea). Avoid yew, which looks similar and is very toxic.
- Blackberries, raspberries, blueberries: aggregate and blue berries are among the safest.

THE UNIVERSAL EDIBILITY TEST
Military manuals describe a slow test (touching, holding in the mouth, then eating small amounts over 24+ hours). It takes a long time, does not detect every toxin, and does not work for mushrooms. Use it only in true long-term emergencies with no other option.

COOKING HELPS
Boiling breaks down some toxins and kills parasites. Throw away the water if a plant is bitter.

INSECTS, FISHING AND SMALL GAME

INSECTS
Insects are high in protein and fat and easy to catch.
- Good choices: grasshoppers, crickets, ants, termites, beetle grubs (in rotten logs), earthworms.
- Avoid: brightly colored insects, hairy caterpillars, anything that stings or bites (except ants once cooked), flies, ticks and mosquitoes.
- Cook them: roast or boil. Remove legs and wings from grasshoppers. Cooking kills parasites.

FISHING
- Improvised hooks: bent safety pins, wire, thorns, carved bone or hardwood.
- Line: fishing line from your kit, inner strands of paracord, dental floss.
- Bait: insects, worms, small pieces of fish guts.
- Fish in the early morning and evening, in shade, near logs and in deeper pools below rapids.
- Set several lines at once and check them often, rather than holding one rod.
- A fish trap: a ring of stones in shallow water with a narrow funnel entrance. Fish swim in and struggle to find their way out.

WARNING: Cook all freshwater fish thoroughly. Avoid fish that look sick, smell bad, or have slimy gills or sunken eyes.

SHELLFISH
- Freshwater mussels and crayfish are good, cooked well.
- Seashore shellfish can carry toxins from algae blooms (red tide), which cooking does not destroy. Avoid during warnings or in warm months if unsure.

SMALL GAME
Snares and traps work, but take skill and time, and are illegal in many places outside emergencies. Check many traps over a wide area. Always cook wild meat until it is no longer pink.

KEEPING FOOD AND CAMP SAFE

KEEP ANIMALS AWAY
- Cook and eat at least 60 m (200 ft) downwind from where you sleep.
- Hang food, trash and anything with a smell (toothpaste, soap) from a branch at least 4 m (12 ft) up and 1.5 m (5 ft) out from the trunk, or use a bear canister.
- Never keep food in your shelter in bear country.

AVOID FOOD POISONING
- Wash hands with soap, or use sanitizer, before handling food.
- Boil water used for cooking and washing dishes.
- Eat cooked food right away. In warm weather, cooked food left out for more than 2 hours can make you sick.
- Throw away cans that bulge, leak, spurt when opened, or smell bad.

PRESERVING MEAT AND FISH
- Drying: cut into thin strips, no thicker than 6 mm (1/4 in), and dry in sun and wind or over a low, smoky fire until brittle.
- Smoking: keep strips in heavy smoke over a low fire for many hours. Smoke keeps insects off and helps preserve.
- Cold: in winter, freeze meat outside in a shaded, animal-proof place.

WARNING: Thin strips and complete drying are key. Thick or damp meat spoils quickly.

EMERGENCY FOOD STORAGE

HOW MUCH
At least 3 days of food for everyone in the home. Two weeks is better. Include pets.

WHAT TO STORE
Choose food your family already eats, that needs little or no cooking and little water:
- Canned meat, fish, beans, soups, vegetables and fruit
- Peanut butter, nuts, trail mix
- Crackers, granola and energy bars
- Dried fruit
- Ready-to-eat cereal, instant oats
- Shelf-stable milk or powdered milk
- Rice, pasta and dried beans (only if you can cook with water to spare)
- Comfort food: chocolate, coffee, tea, sweets. Morale matters.
- Baby food and formula if you have an infant; special foods for anyone with allergies or medical needs

DO NOT FORGET
- A manual can opener
- Plates, cups and utensils you can use without washing
- A way to cook without power: camp stove and fuel, used outdoors only

KEEPING IT FRESH
- Store in a cool, dry, dark place, off the floor.
- First in, first out: use the oldest food first and put new purchases at the back.
- Check dates twice a year (for example, when the clocks change).
- Throw out cans that are bulging, leaking, rusted deeply or dented along a seam.

WARNING: Salty foods make you thirsty. If water is short, choose low-salt foods and canned foods packed in water or juice.

=== NAVIGATION ===

SHADOW-STICK METHOD

This works anywhere in the world on a sunny day.

[Picture: Shadow-stick method]

STEPS
1. Push a straight stick, about 1 m (3 ft) long, upright into flat ground.
2. Mark the tip of its shadow with a small stone. This is your west mark.
3. Wait 15 minutes or more. The shadow tip moves.
4. Mark the new shadow tip. This is your east mark.
5. Draw a line from the first mark to the second: that line runs west to east.
6. Stand with the first mark on your left and the second on your right. You are now facing north.

TIPS
- The longer you wait between marks, the more accurate the result.
- It is most accurate around midday and near the equinoxes, and less accurate early and late in the day.
- At local noon, the shadow is at its shortest and points roughly north (in the Northern Hemisphere) or south (in the Southern Hemisphere), away from the sun.

See also: Finding South with a Watch

FINDING DIRECTION WITH A WATCH

[Picture: Watch method]

NORTHERN HEMISPHERE
1. Hold the watch flat.
2. Point the hour hand at the sun.
3. Halfway between the hour hand and the 12 is south.
4. During daylight saving time, use the 1 instead of the 12.

SOUTHERN HEMISPHERE
1. Point the 12 at the sun.
2. Halfway between the 12 and the hour hand is north.
3. During daylight saving time, use the 1 instead of the 12.

NO ANALOG WATCH?
Draw a clock face on paper or the ground with the hands showing the current time, and use it the same way.

WARNING: This method is a rough guide, off by 20 degrees or more in some places and times, and it does not work well near the equator. Check it against other methods.
