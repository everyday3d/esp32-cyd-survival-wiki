FILTERS
A good pump or squeeze filter removes bacteria and parasites. Most do not stop viruses. In doubt, filter and then disinfect.

SUNLIGHT (SODIS)
- Fill clear plastic (PET) bottles, 2 liters or smaller, with clear water.
- Lay them in full sun for 6 hours, or 2 days if it is cloudy.
- Does not work with cloudy water or in rain.

See also: Improvised Gravity Filter

IMPROVISED GRAVITY FILTER

A layered filter removes mud, debris and some taste. It does not make water safe on its own. Always boil or disinfect after filtering.

[Picture: Layers of an improvised filter]

WHAT YOU NEED
- A container with a small hole in the bottom: plastic bottle with the bottom cut off, a bucket, or a cloth tied into a cone
- Cloth or grass
- Fine sand
- Charcoal from your fire (not ash), crushed into small pieces
- Small gravel or pebbles

BUILDING IT
1. If using a bottle, cut off the bottom and turn it upside down. Plug the neck loosely with cloth.
2. Add layers from the bottom up: cloth, small gravel, fine sand, crushed charcoal, fine sand, then cloth or grass on top.
3. Make each layer several centimeters thick. More sand and charcoal means clearer water.
4. Pour water in slowly. Throw away the first batch; it will carry dust from the charcoal.
5. Run cloudy water through two or three times if needed.

WARNING: Charcoal from a campfire is not the same as activated charcoal. It helps with taste and color, but it does not remove chemicals or germs reliably.

AFTER FILTERING
Boil, use bleach, or use tablets. See Making Water Safe to Drink.

SOLAR STILL

A solar still uses the sun to draw moisture from soil and plants. It makes small amounts, but the water is distilled and clean.

[Picture: Cross-section of a solar still]

BUILDING IT
1. Dig a bowl-shaped hole about 90 cm (3 ft) wide and 60 cm (2 ft) deep, in a sunny spot with damp soil if possible.
2. Put a container in the center at the bottom.
3. Add green, non-poisonous plants around the container (not in it) to add moisture.
4. Cover the hole with clear plastic. Seal the edges with soil and rocks so no air escapes.
5. Put a small rock in the middle of the plastic so it forms a cone right above the container.
6. Water condenses on the plastic, runs down to the low point and drips into the container.

TIPS
- Use a drinking tube from the container to the outside so you do not need to open the still.
- You can pour salt water or urine into the pit (not the container). The still separates clean water.
- Build several stills if you rely on them.

WARNING: A still often yields well under a liter per day, and digging makes you sweat. In hot deserts, do the work at dawn or dusk, and only if there is no better source.

DEHYDRATION AND WATER NEEDS

HOW MUCH YOU NEED
- Resting in mild weather: about 2-3 liters a day.
- Heat or hard work: 4-6 liters or more.
- Cold and high altitude also dry you out faster than you expect.

WARNING: Do not ration water you already have. Drink what you need and work to find more. People have died of dehydration with water still in their bottles.

SIGNS OF DEHYDRATION
- Early: thirst, dark yellow urine, dry mouth, headache, tiredness.
- Worse: little or no urine, dizziness, fast heartbeat, confusion.
- Severe: fainting, no sweat in heat, sunken eyes. This is an emergency.

Check your urine. Pale yellow means you are getting enough.

SAVE WATER INSIDE YOUR BODY
- Rest in the shade during the hottest hours. Travel at night or early morning.
- Keep clothes on in the sun; they slow sweating.
- Breathe through your nose and talk less.
- Avoid alcohol, caffeine and salty or very high-protein food when water is short.
- If water is very limited, eat little. Digestion uses water.

REHYDRATING
- Sip steadily rather than gulping large amounts.
- If someone has been sweating heavily, add a pinch of salt and a little sugar to a liter of water. Commercial oral rehydration salts are best.

STORING WATER AT HOME

HOW MUCH
- At least 4 liters (1 gallon) per person per day: about half for drinking, half for cooking and washing.
- Store at least 3 days' worth. Two weeks is better for home.
- Add more for hot climates, children, nursing mothers, sick people and pets.

Example: a family of 4 for 2 weeks needs about 56 gallons (210 liters).

CONTAINERS
- Best: sealed, store-bought bottled water. Keep it until the expiry date.
- Food-grade water containers (look for "food grade" or the recycle symbol 1, 2 or 4).
- Clean 2-liter soda bottles work well.
- Do not use milk or juice jugs (they hold bacteria and break down), or containers that held chemicals.

FILLING YOUR OWN CONTAINERS
1. Wash the container with soap and hot water, and rinse.
2. Sanitize: swish a solution of 1 teaspoon of unscented bleach in 1 liter (1 quart) of water around the inside, then empty it.
3. Fill with clean tap water. If your water is not chlorinated (for example, a well), add 2 drops of plain unscented bleach per liter.
4. Cap tightly without touching the inside of the cap.
5. Label it with the date.

KEEPING IT GOOD
- Store in a cool, dark place, away from fuels and chemicals (plastic lets fumes through).
- Replace home-filled water every 6 months.

HIDDEN WATER IN YOUR HOME
In an emergency you can also use:
- The water heater tank (turn off the power or gas first, open the drain at the bottom)
- Water in pipes: open the highest tap in the house, then collect from the lowest
- Ice cubes
- The toilet tank (not the bowl), only if no chemicals or cleaning tablets are in it, and treat it first

WARNING: Treat any of these before drinking. See Making Water Safe to Drink. Never drink from waterbeds, pools or radiators.

=== FIRE ===

FIRE BASICS

Fire gives warmth, light, safe water, cooked food, a signal and a big morale boost. Make it before you need it badly.

[Picture: The fire triangle]

WHAT A FIRE NEEDS
- Heat to start it.
- Fuel to burn.
- Oxygen. Do not pack wood too tightly. A fire that is smothered goes out.

THREE SIZES OF FUEL
[Picture: Tinder, kindling and fuel]

- Tinder catches a spark or flame: dry grass, birch bark, cedar bark shredded fine, pine needles, cattail fluff, cotton, paper, wood shavings.
- Kindling carries the flame: dry twigs from match to pencil thickness.
- Fuel keeps it going: sticks and logs from thumb to wrist thickness, then larger.

WARNING: Gather all three sizes before you strike a light. A new fire dies in the time it takes to go looking for more sticks.

FINDING DRY WOOD IN WET WEATHER
- Snap test: dry wood breaks with a clean crack. If it bends, it is too wet.
- Take dead branches still on standing trees, not from the ground.
- Look under thick evergreens, inside rotten stumps and under overhangs.
- Split wet sticks; the center is often dry.
- Shave thin curls (feather sticks) from dry wood for kindling.
- Resin-rich pine wood (fatwood) burns even when damp.

BUILDING UP
Light the tinder, then add kindling a few pieces at a time. Add larger fuel only when the smaller sizes are burning strongly. Blow gently at the base if it struggles.

FIRE LAYS

[Picture: Teepee and log cabin fire lays]

TEEPEE
Put tinder in the center and lean kindling around it in a cone. Leave a gap on the windward side to light it. Burns hot and fast; good for starting and for boiling water.

LOG CABIN
Start with a small teepee, then stack pairs of sticks around it, each layer at right angles, like a cabin. Gives steady heat and a good bed of coals for cooking.

LEAN-TO FIRE
Push a green stick into the ground at an angle over the tinder, or lay one thick log on the windward side. Lean kindling against it. The log shelters the start from wind.

STAR FIRE
Lay several logs like the spokes of a wheel with their ends meeting in the fire. Push them in as they burn. Saves fuel and lasts all night.

DAKOTA FIRE HOLE
Dig a hole about 30 cm (1 ft) deep and wide for the fire. Dig a second hole beside it, on the windward side, and join them with a tunnel at the bottom for air. Burns hot, uses little wood, is hard to see and handles wind well.

WARNING: In snow, build the fire on a platform of green logs or rocks so it does not melt into the snow and drown.

WAYS TO START A FIRE

Carry at least two ways to make fire. Friction methods are a last resort, not a plan.

LIGHTER AND MATCHES
- A cheap disposable lighter is the most reliable tool. Keep one in a warm pocket; cold lighters fail.
- Waterproof your matches in a sealed container. Strike away from your body and shield the flame with your hands.

FERRO ROD (FIRE STEEL)
1. Place the rod tip right in the tinder.
2. Hold the striker still and pull the rod back hard, so sparks fall into the tinder without knocking it apart.
3. Use very fine, dry tinder: cotton with petroleum jelly, cattail fluff, fine bark shavings.

BATTERY AND STEEL WOOL
Touch both terminals of a 9-volt battery to fine steel wool, or bridge a flashlight battery with a strand. The wool glows and lights tinder. Keep steel wool away from batteries when packing.
