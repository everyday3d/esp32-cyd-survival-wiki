Anaphylaxis is a fast, life-threatening allergic reaction, usually to stings, foods, medicines or latex. It can kill within minutes.

SIGNS (OFTEN WITHIN MINUTES OF CONTACT)
- Trouble breathing, wheezing, noisy breathing, tight throat, hoarse voice
- Swelling of the lips, tongue or face
- Hives or widespread rash, flushing
- Dizziness, fainting, pale and floppy (especially in children)
- Vomiting, stomach cramps
- A feeling that something terrible is happening

WHAT TO DO
1. Use an epinephrine auto-injector right away if the person has one. Do not wait to see if it gets worse.
2. Inject into the outer middle of the thigh. It can go through clothing. Follow the instructions printed on the device and hold it in place for the time it says.
3. Call for emergency help, even if they feel better. Symptoms can come back hours later.
4. Positioning:
- Lie them down with legs raised.
- If breathing is hard, let them sit up instead.
- If vomiting or unconscious, put them on their side.
- Do not let them stand or walk suddenly, even if they feel better. This can cause collapse.
5. If there is no improvement after 5 minutes and a second auto-injector is available, give the second one.
6. If they stop breathing normally, start CPR.

WARNING: Antihistamine tablets and inhalers do not stop anaphylaxis. Epinephrine is the treatment. Give it first; other medicines can come after.

REMOVE THE CAUSE
- Stings: scrape the stinger out sideways with a fingernail or card. Do it quickly.
- Food: do not make them vomit.

PREVENTION
Anyone with a known severe allergy should carry two auto-injectors at all times, and the people with them should know where they are and how to use them.

HEAD INJURIES AND CONCUSSION

AFTER ANY BLOW TO THE HEAD
- If they were knocked out, even briefly, treat it as serious.
- Assume a neck injury too if they fell from a height, were in a crash, or have neck pain, numbness or tingling. Keep the head and neck still. See Broken Bones and Sprains.
- Control scalp bleeding with firm pressure. Scalps bleed a lot even from small cuts. Do not press hard if the skull feels soft or dented; press around the edges instead.

CONCUSSION SIGNS
Headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, blurred vision, ringing ears, memory gaps around the injury, feeling "foggy," trouble concentrating.

DANGER SIGNS - GET EMERGENCY HELP URGENTLY
- Getting more drowsy or hard to wake
- Headache that gets worse and will not go away
- Repeated vomiting
- Confusion, strange behavior, slurred speech
- One pupil larger than the other
- Weakness, numbness or trouble moving on one side
- Seizure
- Clear fluid or blood from the nose or ears
- Bruising behind the ears or around both eyes

CARING FOR SOMEONE WITH A MILD HEAD INJURY
- Rest. No hard physical work, and no more risk of another hit.
- Have someone stay with them for at least the first 24 hours and check on them regularly. If they are asleep and cannot be woken normally, that is an emergency.
- Avoid alcohol.
- If unconscious but breathing, put them in the recovery position (on their side), supporting the head and neck.

WARNING: Older adults and people on blood thinners can bleed inside the skull after even a minor bump. Signs may take hours or days. Watch them closely.

TICKS, INSECT STINGS AND SPIDER BITES

TICKS
Ticks can spread Lyme disease and other illnesses. The sooner you remove one, the lower the risk.

[Picture: Removing a tick]

1. Use fine-tipped tweezers. Grab the tick as close to the skin as possible.
2. Pull straight up with slow, steady pressure. Do not twist or jerk.
3. If mouth parts stay in the skin and you cannot easily remove them, leave them; the skin will heal.
4. Clean the bite and your hands with soap and water or alcohol.
5. Do not use heat, nail polish or petroleum jelly. They make the tick release more fluid into you.

WARNING: Watch for a spreading rash (often a "bull's-eye" ring), fever, chills, aches or tiredness in the next 30 days. Get medical care if they appear and say you were bitten.

Prevention: long sleeves and trousers tucked into socks, repellent, and a full-body tick check every evening, including scalp, armpits, groin and behind knees.

BEE AND WASP STINGS
1. Scrape out a bee stinger quickly with a fingernail or card edge.
2. Wash the area.
3. Cool with a cold, wet cloth to ease swelling and pain.
4. Watch for anaphylaxis: trouble breathing, face or throat swelling, dizziness, hives spreading. See Severe Allergic Reaction.
5. Many stings at once, or a sting inside the mouth or throat, needs urgent help.

SPIDER BITES
Most spider bites are harmless. Clean, cool and watch them.

Get medical care for:
- Widow spiders (shiny black, red hourglass mark): severe muscle cramps, stomach pain, sweating.
- Recluse spiders (brown, violin shape on the back): a bite that turns into a spreading, dark or open sore over hours or days.
- Any bite with spreading redness, fever or feeling unwell.

SCORPIONS
Clean the sting and cool it. Most cause sharp pain only. Get urgent help for children, or for anyone with numbness spreading, twitching, roving eye movements, drooling or trouble breathing.

SEIZURES

DURING A SEIZURE
1. Stay calm and time it.
2. Move hard or sharp objects away. Put something soft under their head.
3. Loosen anything tight around the neck.
4. Do not hold them down and do not put anything in their mouth. They cannot swallow their tongue.
5. Stay with them until it ends.

AFTER IT STOPS
- Roll them onto their side in the recovery position to keep the airway clear.
- Check their breathing.
- They will likely be confused and very tired. Speak calmly, tell them what happened, and let them rest.
- Do not give food or drink until they are fully awake.

GET EMERGENCY HELP IF
- The seizure lasts more than 5 minutes
- Another seizure starts soon after the first
- They do not wake up, or have trouble breathing afterward
- It is their first seizure, or you do not know their history
- It happened in water
- They are injured, pregnant, or have diabetes
- They have a high fever or recent head injury

WARNING: Seizures can also be caused by heat stroke, very low blood sugar, head injury or poisoning. Treat the cause if you can: cool someone overheated, and give sugar to a diabetic once they are awake and can swallow.

DIARRHEA, VOMITING AND REHYDRATION

In survival settings, diarrhea from bad water or food is common, and dehydration from it can be deadly, especially for children and older people.

REHYDRATION DRINK
[Picture: Homemade oral rehydration solution]

- 1 liter of clean, safe water
- 6 level teaspoons of sugar
- 1/2 level teaspoon of salt

Stir until dissolved. Measure carefully: too much salt is harmful. Commercial oral rehydration salts (ORS) are best if you have them.

HOW TO GIVE IT
- Small sips often, every few minutes, even if they are vomiting. Most of it will stay down.
- Adults: aim for at least a cup after each loose stool, on top of normal drinking.
- Children: a quarter to half cup after each loose stool, by spoon if needed.
- Keep breastfeeding babies.
- Once vomiting settles, start eating simple foods: rice, porridge, bread, bananas, soup.

DANGER SIGNS - GET HELP
- Blood in the stool or black, tarry stool
- High fever
- No urine for 8 hours or more, very dark urine
- Very sleepy, confused, or unable to drink
- Sunken eyes, dry mouth, no tears (in children)
- Diarrhea lasting more than 3 days, or vomiting that will not stop for a day

STOP IT SPREADING
- Wash hands with soap after the toilet and before handling food or water.
- Dig toilets at least 60 m (200 ft) from water sources, camp and trails, and 15-20 cm (6-8 in) deep. Cover waste completely.
- Treat all drinking water. See Making Water Safe to Drink.
- Keep a separate cup and towel for the sick person.

EYE INJURIES

DUST OR GRIT IN THE EYE
1. Do not rub it.
2. Blink several times and let tears wash it out.
3. Flush with clean water from the inner corner (nose side) outward.
4. Pull the upper lid down over the lower lashes, which can sweep the speck off.
5. If it is on the white of the eye, lift it gently with the corner of a clean, wet cloth.

CHEMICALS IN THE EYE
Flush immediately with lots of clean water for at least 15-20 minutes, holding the eye open, with the injured eye lower so water does not run into the other eye. Then get medical help.

OBJECT STUCK IN THE EYE
WARNING: Do not try to remove anything stuck in the eye.

1. Keep the person still and calm.
2. Cover the injured eye with a paper cup or similar shield taped in place, without pressing on the eye.
3. Cover the other eye too. Eyes move together, so this keeps the injured eye still.
4. Get medical help urgently.

BLOW TO THE EYE
Cool with a cold cloth for 15 minutes, without pressure. Seek help for vision changes, blood inside the colored part of the eye, severe pain, or if they cannot move the eye normally.
