WARNING: Always make an air vent in any snow shelter and keep it clear. Keep a digging tool inside with you.

TREE WELL
Under a large evergreen, snow is often shallow near the trunk. Dig out the space and roof it with boughs.

BODY HEAT TIPS
- Eat before sleeping; digestion makes heat.
- Do light exercise (not to the point of sweating) before getting in your shelter.
- Put a bottle of hot water inside your clothes or bag.
- Share body heat with others.

DESERT AND HOT WEATHER SHELTER

In heat, shelter means shade. The goal is to save water by not sweating.

SIMPLE SHADE
- Use natural shade: rock overhangs, the shady side of boulders, bushes.
- Hang a tarp or blanket 30-45 cm (12-18 in) above the ground, with a second layer above it if you have it. The air gap between layers cuts heat a lot.

BELOW-GROUND SHELTER
1. Dig a trench 45-60 cm (18-24 in) deep, as long as your body, oriented east-west.
2. Pile the sand around the edges.
3. Cover with a tarp, then a second layer with a gap between them.
The ground 30 cm down can be much cooler than the surface. Dig at dawn or dusk, not midday.

HABITS
- Rest in the heat of the day; work and travel at night or early morning.
- Keep your skin covered with loose, light-colored clothing and a hat.
- Do not lie directly on hot ground. Sit on a pack or brush.
- Watch for snakes and scorpions in shade spots; check before you reach in.

WARNING: Cars, metal sheds and tents in full sun can become deadly hot. Shade with airflow is better.

SLEEPING WARM

A cold night is miserable and dangerous, and it wrecks your judgment the next day. These tricks work with or without good gear.

BEFORE BED
- Eat something, especially fats and sugars. Your body burns food to make heat. Keep a snack by your bed for the middle of the night.
- Drink warm water. Being dehydrated makes you colder.
- Pee before you lie down. Your body wastes heat keeping a full bladder warm.
- Warm up with light exercise (not to the point of sweating) right before getting in.
- Change into dry clothes, especially socks. Put damp clothes in your bag to dry them with body heat if you have no other way.

UNDER YOU MATTERS MOST
The ground pulls heat from you faster than the air. Put down at least 10-20 cm (4-8 in) of dry insulation: leaves, grass, pine boughs, a foam pad, an empty pack, a coiled rope. Then more.

AROUND YOU
- Wear a hat and cover your neck.
- Fill air gaps in your sleeping bag or blankets with spare clothes.
- Fill a bottle with hot water (check the lid seals and the bottle can take heat), wrap it in a sock, and put it between your thighs or on your stomach.
- Keep your face out of the bag; breath adds moisture that makes it colder.
- Huddle together with others if you can.

THROUGH THE NIGHT
- If you wake up cold, eat, do some sit-ups or clench and relax your muscles, then try again.
- Keep tomorrow's water and boots inside your shelter or bag so they do not freeze.

WARNING: Shivering is a warning. If someone shivers hard and cannot stop, or starts stumbling and mumbling, treat for Hypothermia now.

=== FIRST AID ===

FIRST AID PRIORITIES

WARNING: This wiki is a field reference, not medical training. Take a first aid or wilderness first aid course. Get professional care as soon as you can.

1. MAKE THE SCENE SAFE
Check for traffic, fire, falling rocks, water, electricity or an animal. Do not become a second casualty.

2. CHECK THE PERSON
Tap their shoulder and shout. Are they awake and answering?

3. CALL OR SEND FOR HELP
If you have any signal, call your local emergency number (911 in North America, 112 in Europe and many countries). Text messages sometimes get through when calls do not.

4. WORK THROUGH THE BIG KILLERS, IN ORDER
- Massive bleeding: stop it first. See Severe Bleeding.
- Airway: open it with head tilt and chin lift. Clear anything visible in the mouth.
- Breathing: look, listen and feel for up to 10 seconds. Gasping is not normal breathing.
- No normal breathing: start CPR.
- Circulation: look for other bleeding and signs of Shock.

5. THEN CHECK HEAD TO TOE
Look and gently feel for injuries: head, neck, chest, belly, pelvis, arms and legs. Ask what hurts. Note what you find and the time.

RECOVERY POSITION
For someone breathing but not awake (and with no suspected spine injury): roll them onto their side, top knee bent to hold them there, head tilted back slightly so fluids drain from the mouth. Check breathing often.

PROTECT THEM
Keep them warm, off the cold ground, and calm. Do not give food or drink to someone who is badly injured or not fully alert.

CPR

Start CPR if a person is unresponsive and not breathing normally (or only gasping).

[Picture: Hands-only CPR]

ADULTS
1. Call for help. If someone else is there, send them to call and to find an AED (defibrillator).
2. Lay the person on their back on a firm surface.
3. Put the heel of one hand on the center of the chest (lower half of the breastbone), the other hand on top.
4. Arms straight, shoulders over your hands. Push hard and fast:
- Depth: 5-6 cm (2-2.4 in)
- Rate: 100-120 per minute (the beat of the song "Stayin' Alive")
- Let the chest come all the way back up between pushes.
5. If trained and willing: after every 30 compressions, give 2 rescue breaths (head tilt, chin lift, pinch nose, breathe until the chest rises).
6. If not trained: keep doing compressions only. That still saves lives.
7. Keep going until the person starts breathing, an AED is ready, help takes over, or you are too exhausted to continue.

AED
Turn it on and follow the voice instructions. Keep compressions going while it is being set up.

CHILDREN AND INFANTS
- Child: one or two hands, about 5 cm (2 in) deep.
- Infant: two fingers in the center of the chest just below the nipple line, about 4 cm (1.5 in) deep.
- For children and drowning victims, rescue breaths are especially important if you can give them.

WARNING: In remote places with no chance of advanced care, CPR rarely restarts the heart after a long time. It is still right to try. Your safety and the group's safety come first.

SEVERE BLEEDING

Heavy bleeding can kill in minutes. Act fast.

1. DIRECT PRESSURE
- Put a cloth or gauze on the wound (bare hands if nothing else) and press hard with both hands.
- Hold pressure for at least 10 minutes without lifting to check.
- If blood soaks through, add more material on top and keep pressing.

2. PACK DEEP WOUNDS
For a deep wound in the thigh, armpit, groin or neck, push gauze or clean cloth into the wound, filling it, then press hard on top for at least 3 minutes (10 if plain cloth).

3. TOURNIQUET FOR ARMS AND LEGS
Use a tourniquet when bleeding from an arm or leg is life-threatening and pressure does not stop it, or you cannot hold pressure (for example, several injured people).

[Picture: Tourniquet placement]

1. Place it 5-7 cm (2-3 in) above the wound, never on a joint. If unsure, place it high on the limb.
2. Tighten until the bleeding stops. It will hurt a lot. That is expected.
3. Write the time on the tourniquet or the person's skin.
4. Do not loosen it. Leave it for medical staff.
5. If bleeding continues, add a second tourniquet just above the first.

IMPROVISED TOURNIQUET
A strip of cloth at least 4 cm (1.5 in) wide, tied around the limb, with a stick through the knot. Twist the stick until bleeding stops, then tie the stick in place. Thin cord or wire cuts the skin and does not work well.

WARNING: After bleeding is controlled, keep the person warm and lying down. Watch for Shock.

CHOKING

If the person can cough or speak, encourage them to keep coughing. Act when they cannot breathe, cough or speak.

ADULTS AND CHILDREN OVER 1 YEAR
1. Stand behind them. Lean them forward.
2. Give up to 5 firm back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand.
3. If that does not work, give up to 5 abdominal thrusts: make a fist just above the belly button, grab it with your other hand, and pull sharply in and up.
4. Keep alternating 5 back blows and 5 thrusts until the object comes out.
5. Use chest thrusts instead of abdominal thrusts for pregnant women and very large people.

INFANTS (UNDER 1 YEAR)
1. Lay the baby face down along your forearm, head lower than the body, supporting the jaw.
2. Give 5 back blows between the shoulder blades.
3. Turn the baby face up and give 5 chest thrusts with two fingers in the center of the chest.
4. Repeat. Never do abdominal thrusts on an infant.

IF THEY BECOME UNRESPONSIVE
Lower them to the ground and start CPR. Look in the mouth before rescue breaths and remove any object you can see. Do not sweep blindly with your finger.

WARNING: Anyone who received abdominal thrusts should be checked by a medical professional when possible.

SHOCK

Shock happens when the body cannot get enough blood to the organs, often after bleeding, burns, severe pain or dehydration. It can kill even after the injury seems handled.
