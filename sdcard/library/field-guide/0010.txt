MOBILE PHONES
- Try calling your emergency number (911, 112) even with no bars. Emergency calls can use any carrier's tower.
- Text messages need less signal than calls. Many regions accept texts to 911 or 112.
- Climb high or move to open ground for a better signal.
- Save battery: airplane mode, low brightness, and check for signal at set times.
- Some newer phones can send emergency messages by satellite. Check your phone's emergency settings before a trip.

COMMON EMERGENCY RADIO CHANNELS (NORTH AMERICA)
- CB: channel 9 (emergency), channel 19 (truckers)
- FRS/GMRS: channel 1 or 3 are commonly monitored in some areas. No license needed for FRS.
- Amateur (ham) 2 m: 146.520 MHz (national calling frequency). A license is normally required to transmit, but anyone may transmit in a genuine emergency.
- Marine VHF: channel 16 (156.8 MHz), distress and calling
- NOAA Weather Radio: 162.400-162.550 MHz (receive only)

WARNING: Channel use differs by country. In Europe, PMR446 radios are common, and 112 works on most phones.

MAKING A DISTRESS CALL
Say "Mayday, Mayday, Mayday" (life-threatening) or "Pan-pan" (urgent), then: who you are, where you are, what happened, how many people, and what help you need. Repeat, then listen.

PERSONAL LOCATOR BEACONS (PLB)
A PLB or satellite messenger sends your position to rescuers by satellite, anywhere in the world with a view of the sky. It is the most reliable way to call for help in remote areas.

SIGNALING AT NIGHT

At night, even a small light can be seen from far away, especially by aircraft. Night-vision equipment used by many rescue crews makes lights even easier to spot.

FLASHLIGHT OR HEADLAMP
- Flash SOS: three short, three long, three short. See SOS, Whistle, Fire and Smoke.
- Point it at the aircraft or toward searchers' lights. Sweep slowly across them.
- Save batteries: do not leave it on constantly. Signal when you hear or see searchers, and on a schedule (for example, for a minute at the top of every hour).

GLOW STICK ON A CORD
Tie a glow stick to 1 m (3 ft) of cord and swing it in a big circle. It makes a large, moving ring of light that stands out from the ground.

PHONE
- Use the flashlight function to signal.
- A bright white screen is visible surprisingly far in the dark.
- Turn the phone off between signals to save battery.

FIRE
Bright, tall flames show best at night. Keep a signal fire built and ready to light, with fast-burning fuel on top. See SOS, Whistle, Fire and Smoke.

REFLECTORS
Hang anything reflective (space blankets, reflective strips on packs and jackets) around camp. A searcher's flashlight or spotlight will make it shine.

SOUND TRAVELS FURTHER AT NIGHT
Air is often still and quiet at night. Blow your whistle in sets of three and then listen carefully for a reply.

HELPING SEARCHERS FIND YOU

Search teams work in a systematic way. Help them by staying put, being visible, and making noise.

STAY IN ONE PLACE
Searchers look hardest around your last known location and route. A moving person is much harder to find, and may walk out of an area that was already searched.

BE EASY TO SEE
- Go to the most open spot nearby: a clearing, ridge, meadow, beach or riverbank.
- Spread bright clothing, tarps or space blankets in the open.
- Build Ground-to-Air Signals and keep a signal fire ready.
- Use a Signal Mirror on any aircraft you see.

BE EASY TO HEAR
- Blow your whistle in sets of three, then pause and listen.
- Bang metal or rocks if you have no whistle.
- Answer when you hear your name called. Some lost people hide from searchers out of fear or embarrassment. Do not.

LEAVE CLUES IF YOU MOVE
If you must move, leave a note at your last spot with the time, your direction and your condition. Mark your route with arrows and broken branches.

WHEN A HELICOPTER COMES
- Stay in the open and wave both arms in a Y (yes, we need help).
- Do not approach a helicopter unless the crew signals you to. Come from the front, where the pilot can see you, crouching low.
- Never go near the tail rotor. Never approach from uphill, where blades are closer to the ground.
- Tie down or hold onto loose items like tarps, hats and sleeping bags; the downdraft is very strong.
- If you can, clear a landing area about 30 x 30 m (100 x 100 ft), flat, with no wires, tall trees or loose debris.

WHEN SEARCHERS REACH YOU
Tell them: how many people, injuries, medical conditions, and anyone still missing.
