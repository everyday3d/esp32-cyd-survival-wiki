MOOSE AND OTHER LARGE PLANT-EATERS
Moose injure more people than bears in some regions. If ears go back and hair on its hump stands up, it is angry. Run and get behind a tree or vehicle: moose usually stop chasing quickly.

DOGS
- Do not run or stare. Stand still, side-on, hands close to your body.
- Say "No" or "Go home" firmly.
- If attacked, put something between you and the dog (a pack, jacket or stick). If knocked down, curl into a ball and protect your face and neck.

WARNING: Any animal bite or scratch that breaks the skin needs thorough washing for 15 minutes with soap and water, and medical care as soon as possible because of rabies and infection.

ICE SAFETY AND FALLING THROUGH ICE

IS THE ICE SAFE?
Clear, hard, new ice is strongest. White or cloudy ice is weaker, often about half as strong.
- Under 5 cm (2 in): stay off.
- 10 cm (4 in) of clear ice: walking for one person.
- 13-18 cm (5-7 in): a snowmobile or small all-terrain vehicle.
- 20-30 cm (8-12 in): a small car.

Ice is never the same thickness everywhere. It is thinner near inlets, outlets, moving water, docks, rocks, logs and dark spots, and under snow. Check thickness every few steps with an ice chisel, axe or auger.

WARNING: Never go onto ice alone. Carry ice picks (two spikes on a cord around your neck) and a throw rope.

IF YOU FALL IN: THE 1-10-1 RULE
[Picture: The 1-10-1 rule for falling through ice]

- 1 minute: cold shock makes you gasp and breathe fast. Keep your head above water, do not panic, and slow your breathing. It passes.
- 10 minutes: of useful movement before your arms and legs go numb. Use them to get out now.
- 1 hour: before you lose consciousness from hypothermia, even if you cannot get out. Keep your arms on the ice; they may freeze to it and hold you up.

GETTING OUT
1. Turn toward the direction you came from. That ice held you before.
2. Put your arms flat on the ice. Use ice picks, keys or a knife to grip.
3. Kick your legs hard to bring your body up level with the surface, like a swimmer.
4. Pull and kick until your chest and hips are on the ice.
5. Roll away from the hole. Do not stand up until you are on thick ice.
6. Get to shelter and warmth immediately. See Hypothermia and Frostbite.

HELPING SOMEONE ELSE
Reach, throw, don't go. Do not run out to them; you will likely fall in too.
- Call for help.
- Reach with a long pole, branch, rope or ladder while lying flat or standing on solid ice or shore.
- Throw a rope or anything that floats.
- If you must go out, lie flat to spread your weight and push something ahead of you for them to grab.

STAYING CALM AND KEEPING MORALE

Survival is mostly mental. People with very little gear have survived because they kept their heads. People with plenty have died because they panicked.

CALM YOURSELF IN A MINUTE
Box breathing: breathe in for 4 seconds, hold for 4, breathe out for 4, hold for 4. Repeat four or five times. It slows your heart and helps you think.

Then use STOP: Stop, Think, Observe, Plan.

KEEP GOING
- Give yourself small goals. "Get the shelter roof done before dark" is easier than "survive."
- Keep a routine: set times to check signals, collect water and firewood, eat and sleep.
- Stay busy. Useful work keeps fear down.
- Look after your body. Cold, thirst, hunger and lack of sleep all make your thinking worse. Fix them early.
- Keep a log. Write the date, what you did, and what you plan. It helps you think, and helps rescuers later.

IN A GROUP
- Choose one leader to make final decisions, and listen to everyone first.
- Give every person a job, even children and injured people. Feeling useful matters.
- Check on each other. Watch for anyone who goes quiet, stops eating or wanders off.
- Keep the group together. Never let anyone leave camp alone without saying where and when they will be back.

WITH CHILDREN
- Stay calm in front of them. They take their cues from you.
- Explain simply and honestly what is happening and what the plan is.
- Give them simple tasks and praise.
- Teach them: if you are lost, stay put, and hug a tree. Make noise, answer anyone calling your name, and do not hide from searchers.

WARNING: Rescue usually comes. Most people who are reported missing are found within a day or two.

HOME EMERGENCIES AND POWER OUTAGES

Most emergencies happen at home: storms, floods, winter outages, wildfires. A little planning makes a huge difference.

FAMILY EMERGENCY PLAN
- Pick two meeting places: one near home, one outside your neighborhood.
- Choose an out-of-area contact person everyone calls or texts. Long-distance lines often work when local ones are jammed.
- Write down important phone numbers on paper. Phones die.
- Know how to turn off your water, gas and electricity.
- Plan for pets, older family members and anyone with medical needs.

HOME SUPPLIES (AT LEAST 3 DAYS, IDEALLY 2 WEEKS)
- Water: about 4 liters (1 gallon) per person per day. See Storing Water at Home.
- Food that needs no cooking or little water. See Emergency Food Storage.
- Flashlights, headlamps and spare batteries (avoid candles if you can)
- A battery or hand-crank radio
- First aid kit and at least a week of any prescription medicines
- Phone power banks
- Warm clothing, blankets or sleeping bags
- Cash in small bills
- Copies of important documents in a waterproof bag

DURING A POWER OUTAGE
- Keep the fridge and freezer closed. A closed fridge stays cold for about 4 hours; a full freezer about 48 hours (24 if half full).
- Unplug electronics to protect them from a surge when power returns. Leave one light switched on so you know when it is back.
- In cold weather, close off unused rooms, gather in one room, cover windows with blankets and wear layers.
- In hot weather, stay in the lowest, shadiest room and drink plenty of water.

WARNING: Generators, grills and camp stoves must never be used indoors, including in garages, basements and on porches. Run generators at least 6 m (20 ft) from any window, door or vent. Fit a battery carbon monoxide alarm.

FOOD SAFETY AFTER AN OUTAGE
Throw out perishable food (meat, fish, dairy, eggs, leftovers) that has been above 4 C (40 F) for more than 2 hours. When in doubt, throw it out. Never taste food to check it.

IF TOLD TO EVACUATE
Go early. Take your emergency kit, medicines, documents, phone chargers and pets. Follow the routes officials give; shortcuts may be blocked or dangerous.

=== WATER ===

FINDING WATER

WHERE TO LOOK
- Follow the land downhill. Water collects in valleys, gullies and low ground.
- Green vegetation such as willows, cottonwoods, reeds and cattails usually means water is near or just under the surface.
- Animal trails often lead to water, especially where several trails join and head downhill.
- Birds circling or flying low and straight at dawn and dusk are often heading to water.
- Insects: bees and flies usually stay within a few kilometers of water.
- Dry stream beds: dig on the outside of bends, where the last water would sit. If the sand is damp, dig a hole and wait for water to seep in.

COLLECTING WATER
- Rain: spread a tarp, poncho or plastic sheet and funnel it into a container. Rainwater is the cleanest natural source.
- Dew: at dawn, wipe grass with a cloth and wring it out.
- Snow and ice: melt it first. Eating snow lowers your body temperature. Ice gives more water per pot than snow.
- Transpiration bag: tie a clear plastic bag over a leafy, non-poisonous branch in the sun. Water condenses inside.

WARNING: Treat all water from the ground, streams, lakes, snow and dew before drinking. Even clear mountain water can carry parasites.

WATER TO AVOID
- Seawater and urine: they dehydrate you faster.
- Water near mines, factories, farm runoff, or with dead animals nearby.
- Water with oily film, strong smell or no living plants around it.

MAKING WATER SAFE TO DRINK

Clear water can still make you very sick. Use one of these methods. If the water is cloudy, let it settle and pour it through cloth first.

BOILING (MOST RELIABLE)
- Bring water to a rolling boil for 1 minute.
- Above 2,000 m (6,500 ft), boil for 3 minutes.
- Let it cool covered. Boiling kills germs but does not remove chemicals.

HOUSEHOLD BLEACH
Use plain, unscented bleach (no added cleaners or scent). Check the strength on the label.

- 6% bleach: 8 drops per 4 liters (1 gallon), 2 drops per liter
- 8.25% bleach: 6 drops per 4 liters (1 gallon), 2 drops per liter
- If the water is cloudy or very cold, double the dose.
- Stir and wait 30 minutes. It should smell slightly of chlorine. If not, repeat the dose and wait 15 more minutes.

TABLETS AND DROPS
Chlorine dioxide, iodine or chlorine tablets work well. Follow the package exactly, especially the waiting time. Chlorine dioxide also kills tough parasites like Cryptosporidium if you wait the full time (often 4 hours).

WARNING: Iodine is not suitable for pregnant women, people with thyroid problems, or for use over many weeks.
