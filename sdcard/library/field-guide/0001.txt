=== FIRST STEPS ===

THE RULE OF 3S

In an emergency, deal with the biggest threat first. The Rule of 3s is a rough guide to how long a person can last without each basic need.

[Picture: The Rule of 3s]

THE ORDER
1. Air - about 3 minutes. Breathing problems, choking, drowning, smoke and severe bleeding come first.
2. Shelter - about 3 hours in harsh weather. Cold, wet and wind kill faster than thirst. Stay dry and insulated.
3. Water - about 3 days. Less in heat or with hard work.
4. Food - about 3 weeks. Food matters for morale and energy, but it is the last priority.

WARNING: These are averages, not promises. Cold water, heat, injury, age and illness shorten every limit.

USING IT
- Before you build anything, check for immediate danger: fire, rising water, traffic, falling rocks.
- Treat life-threatening injuries next.
- Then protect yourself from the weather, then find water.
- Spend energy on food only once the first three are handled.

See also: STOP: Stop, Think, Observe, Plan

STOP - STOP, THINK, OBSERVE, PLAN

Most survival deaths come from bad decisions made in the first hour. When things go wrong, use STOP.

S - STOP
Sit down. Drink some water, eat something small. Do not keep walking. Panic burns energy and makes you more lost.

T - THINK
- What happened, and what do you actually have?
- Does anyone know where you are and when you are due back?
- What is the biggest danger right now?

O - OBSERVE
- Weather: is it getting colder, darker, wetter?
- Terrain: water sources, shelter material, open areas for signaling.
- Your body: injuries, warmth, thirst, how much daylight is left.
- Your gear: empty your pockets and pack. Everything counts.

P - PLAN
Decide on one clear plan and follow it. Re-check it every few hours.

WARNING: If someone knows your route and expected return, it is usually best to stay put. Searchers find people who stay in one place far more often.

STAY OR GO?
Stay if: people will look for you, you are injured, the weather is bad, or you do not know which way to go.

Move only if: no one will search for you, your location is unsafe, or you know a clear route to help. If you move, leave a note or arrow showing your direction.

BASIC SURVIVAL KIT

A small kit you actually carry beats a big kit left at home. Build it around the Rule of 3s.

CORE ITEMS
- Fire: lighter, ferro rod, waterproof matches, tinder (cotton balls with petroleum jelly)
- Shelter: emergency blanket or bivy bag, small tarp, 15 m of cord
- Water: metal bottle you can boil in, purification tablets, filter
- Light: headlamp with spare batteries
- Signal: whistle, signal mirror, bright cloth
- Tools: fixed-blade or folding knife, duct tape
- First aid: bandages, gauze, tape, tourniquet, pain relievers, personal medicines
- Navigation: map of the area, compass
- Food: high-calorie bars

CLOTHING
- Warm layer (wool or synthetic, not cotton)
- Rain jacket
- Hat and gloves
- Spare socks

WARNING: Cotton loses its warmth when wet. In cold or wet weather, wool and synthetics can be the difference between cold and hypothermic.

TELL SOMEONE
The most important item is not in the kit: leave your route and expected return time with someone you trust.

LIGHTNING AND WEATHER DANGERS

LIGHTNING
- If you can hear thunder, you are close enough to be struck. Get to a building or hard-topped vehicle if you can.
- Avoid summits, ridges, open fields, lone trees, water and metal fences.
- In a forest, go to a low area among smaller trees of similar height.
- Groups should spread out at least 15 m (50 ft) apart so one strike does not hit everyone.
- Wait 30 minutes after the last thunder before going back out.

WARNING: There is no truly safe place outdoors in a thunderstorm. If caught in the open with no option, crouch low on the balls of your feet, feet together, away from tall objects.

FLASH FLOODS
- Never camp in a dry stream bed or narrow canyon.
- Rain far upstream can cause a flood where the sky is clear.
- Do not walk or drive through moving water. 15 cm (6 in) of fast water can knock you down; 60 cm (2 ft) can float a car.
- Climb to higher ground if you hear a roar or see water rising and muddying.

WIND AND COLD FRONTS
- A sudden temperature drop, shifting wind and dark clouds mean bad weather is near. Get shelter set up early.
- Wind steals body heat fast. Block the wind before anything else.

ESSENTIAL KNOTS

Learn a few knots well rather than many badly. Practice them until you can tie them with cold hands or in the dark.

BOWLINE - A LOOP THAT WILL NOT SLIP
Use it for a fixed loop: tying a line around a tree, a rescue loop, hanging a food bag.
1. Make a small loop in the standing part of the rope, with the working end lying on top.
2. Pass the working end up through the small loop from underneath.
3. Take it around behind the standing part.
4. Bring it back down through the small loop.
5. Hold the working end against the big loop and pull the standing part to tighten.

WARNING: Leave a tail at least 10 cm (4 in) long. A bowline can loosen when it is not under load, so check it.

CLOVE HITCH - QUICK TIE TO A POLE
Use it to start lashings or tie a line to a post.
1. Wrap the rope once around the pole.
2. Cross over the first wrap and go around again.
3. Tuck the end under the second wrap, so both ends come out between the wraps in opposite directions.
4. Pull both ends tight.

TAUT-LINE HITCH - AN ADJUSTABLE TENT LINE
Lets you tighten or loosen a guy line without retying.
1. Pass the rope around a stake or anchor.
2. Wrap the working end twice around the standing part, working back toward the anchor, inside the loop.
3. Make one more wrap around the standing part on the outside of those two wraps.
4. Tighten. Slide the knot to adjust the tension. It holds under load.

SQUARE (REEF) KNOT - TIE TWO ENDS TOGETHER
For bandages, bundles and packages.
1. Right end over left, and tuck under.
2. Left end over right, and tuck under.
3. Both ends should lie flat, alongside their own parts.

WARNING: Do not use a square knot to join two ropes that will carry weight. It can slip or spill. Use a sheet bend.

SHEET BEND - JOIN TWO ROPES
Works even with ropes of different thickness.
1. Make a bend (U shape) in the end of the thicker rope.
2. Pass the thinner rope up through the bend from behind.
3. Wrap it around behind both sides of the bend.
4. Tuck it under itself, not back through the bend. Pull tight.

TRUCKER'S HITCH - PULL A LINE VERY TIGHT
Great for ridgelines and tying down loads.
1. Tie one end to an anchor.
2. Partway along the line, twist a small loop into the rope (a slip loop works).
3. Run the free end around the second anchor and back up through that loop.
4. Pull down on the free end. The loop acts like a pulley and gives you about twice the pull.
5. Hold the tension and lock it off with two half hitches below the loop.

ROPE CARE
- Melt or tape the ends of synthetic cord so they do not fray.
- Keep rope out of fire and away from sharp edges.
- Paracord can be pulled apart: the inner strands make fishing line, sewing thread or snare line.

DANGEROUS ANIMAL ENCOUNTERS

Most wild animals avoid people. Encounters usually happen when an animal is surprised, defending young or food, or used to human food.

AVOID THEM
- Make noise in thick brush and near streams so animals hear you coming.
- Never leave food, trash or toiletries in your shelter. See Keeping Food and Camp Safe.
- Travel in groups during the day. Keep children close.
- Give all animals space, especially mothers with young.
- Carry bear spray in bear country, where you can reach it in a second.

BEARS - GENERAL RULES
- Do not run. Bears are much faster than people, and running can trigger a chase.
- Stay calm and talk in a low, firm voice so it knows you are human.
- Pick up small children. Stand together as a group.
- Back away slowly, sideways, while watching the bear.
- If it approaches, use bear spray when it is about 10 m (30 ft) away: aim slightly down so the cloud is between you and the bear.

IF A BEAR ATTACKS
Defensive attack (surprised bear, often a grizzly or brown bear, or a mother with cubs):
- Play dead. Lie flat on your stomach, hands clasped behind your neck, legs apart so it is hard to flip you. Keep your pack on.
- Stay still until the bear has left the area.
- If it keeps attacking for a long time, fight back hard.

Predatory attack (a bear stalks you, follows you, or attacks at night in your tent - often a black bear):
- Fight back with everything you have. Hit the face and nose with rocks, sticks, anything.
- Do not play dead.

COUGARS (MOUNTAIN LIONS)
- Do not run, and do not turn your back.
- Make yourself look big: raise your arms, open your jacket, stand on a rock.
- Shout, throw things, and back away slowly.
- If attacked, fight back and protect your neck. Stay on your feet if you can.
