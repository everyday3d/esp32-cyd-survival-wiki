SIGNS
- Pale, cool, clammy skin
- Fast, weak pulse and fast breathing
- Anxiety, restlessness or confusion
- Thirst, nausea
- Later: drowsiness and unresponsiveness

WHAT TO DO
1. Stop any bleeding.
2. Lay the person down on their back.
3. If there is no injury to the head, neck, back, hips or legs, you may raise the legs about 30 cm (12 in).
4. Keep them warm. Put insulation under them and cover them. Cold makes shock worse.
5. Keep them calm and reassure them.
6. Do not give food or drink if they may need surgery soon. In a remote setting where help is many hours away and the person is fully alert, small sips of water are reasonable.
7. Check breathing and pulse often. Start CPR if they stop breathing normally.

WARNING: If vomiting or not fully awake, turn them onto their side to protect the airway.

HYPOTHERMIA AND FROSTBITE

Hypothermia can happen well above freezing when someone is wet, tired and in the wind.

SIGNS - WATCH FOR THE "UMBLES"
- Mild: shivering, stumbling, fumbling hands, mumbling, poor decisions.
- Moderate to severe: shivering slows or stops, confusion, drowsiness, slurred speech, acting strangely (some people even undress).

TREATMENT
1. Get them out of the wind and wet. Shelter first.
2. Replace wet clothes with dry ones, or cover wet clothes with a waterproof layer if you cannot change them.
3. Insulate from the ground, and wrap the whole body, including the head and neck.
4. Put warm (not hot) water bottles or heat packs, wrapped in cloth, on the chest, armpits and back.
5. If fully awake and able to swallow: give warm, sweet drinks and food.
6. Share body heat in a sleeping bag if needed.

WARNING: Handle a severely cold person very gently and keep them lying flat. Rough movement can trigger a fatal heart rhythm. Do not rub the skin. No alcohol.

If they are unresponsive and not breathing normally after checking for up to a full minute, start CPR.

FROSTBITE
- Signs: numb, white or grey-yellow skin that feels hard or waxy. Fingers, toes, nose, ears.
- Do not rub it, and do not use direct heat like a fire or stove.
- Only rewarm if it will not freeze again. Refreezing does much more damage.
- Rewarm in warm water about 37-39 C (99-102 F), comfortable to your own hand, for 15-30 minutes. It will be very painful.
- Do not pop blisters. Cover loosely with dry, clean dressing and put gauze between fingers and toes.

HEAT EXHAUSTION AND HEAT STROKE

HEAT EXHAUSTION
Signs: heavy sweating, pale or flushed skin, weakness, dizziness, headache, nausea, cramps, fast pulse.

1. Move to shade and rest.
2. Loosen and remove extra clothing.
3. Cool with wet cloths and fanning.
4. Give water or a salt-and-sugar drink in small sips.
5. Rest for the rest of the day. If not better within an hour, treat as heat stroke.

HEAT STROKE (LIFE-THREATENING)
Signs: very hot body, confusion, odd behavior, slurred speech, seizure or collapse. Skin may be dry or still sweating.

WARNING: Any confusion in a hot, overheated person is heat stroke until proven otherwise. Cool first, transport second.

1. Cool them immediately and aggressively.
2. Best: put them in cool or cold water up to the neck (a stream, lake or tub), keeping the head above water and staying with them.
3. If you cannot: soak them with water and fan hard. Put cold packs or wet cloths on the neck, armpits and groin. Keep re-wetting.
4. Keep cooling until they are thinking clearly again.
5. Do not give drinks to someone who is confused or not fully awake.
6. Get medical help. Heat stroke can damage organs even after recovery.

PREVENTION
Drink regularly, rest in the shade, work in the cool hours, and watch each other for early signs.

BURNS

STOP THE BURNING
- If clothing is on fire: stop, drop and roll, or smother with a blanket.
- Remove rings, watches and tight clothing near the burn before it swells. Do not pull off clothing stuck to the skin.

COOL IT
- Cool with clean, cool running water for 20 minutes (at least 10). This helps even if started late, up to about 3 hours after.
- Do not use ice, butter, oil, toothpaste or ointment on a fresh burn.
- Keep the rest of the person warm. Cooling a large burn can cause hypothermia, especially in children.

COVER IT
- Cover loosely with clean plastic wrap (laid on, not wrapped tight), a clean non-stick dressing, or a clean plastic bag over a hand.
- Do not pop blisters.

SERIOUS BURNS - GET HELP
- Larger than the person's palm, or deep (white, leathery or charred)
- On the face, hands, feet, genitals or over joints
- Electrical or chemical burns
- Burns in the airway: soot around the nose or mouth, hoarse voice, coughing, trouble breathing. These can swell shut. Urgent.

CHEMICAL BURNS
Brush off dry chemicals, then flush with lots of water for at least 20 minutes.

WARNING: Large burns cause dangerous fluid loss. Watch for Shock and give sips of fluid if the person is alert and help is far away.

BROKEN BONES AND SPRAINS

SIGNS OF A BREAK
Pain, swelling, bruising, deformity, grating, or the person cannot use the limb. If in doubt, treat it as broken.

SPLINTING
1. Control any bleeding first. Cover open wounds and bone ends with a clean dressing. Do not push bone back in.
2. Check circulation, feeling and movement beyond the injury (fingers or toes): warm, pink, can feel your touch, can wiggle.
3. Splint the limb in the position you find it, unless it is badly bent and there is no pulse beyond it (then gently straighten it once, along its length, if trained).
4. Immobilize the joint above and below a broken bone, or the bones above and below an injured joint.
5. Use anything rigid: sticks, trekking poles, rolled magazines, a sleeping pad. Pad well with clothing.
6. Tie in place firmly but not tightly, with knots away from the injury.
7. Re-check circulation after splinting and every 30 minutes. If fingers or toes go pale, cold or numb, loosen the ties.

SLINGS
For arm, wrist and collarbone injuries, support the arm in a sling made from a triangle of cloth or a shirt, with the hand slightly higher than the elbow.

SPRAINS
Rest, cool packs wrapped in cloth for 20 minutes at a time, gentle compression wrap, and raise the limb. A sprained ankle may be walkable with a firm wrap and a stick if you must move.

WARNING: Suspect a spine injury after a fall, crash or blow to the head with neck or back pain, numbness or tingling. Keep the head and neck still and do not move the person unless they are in danger.

WOUND CARE AND INFECTION

In the wild, infection is a bigger long-term danger than most cuts.

CLEANING
1. Stop the bleeding with pressure.
2. Irrigate with lots of drinkable water: at least 1 liter for a wound. Squirt it with force, for example from a bag with a pinhole or a syringe.
3. Remove dirt and splinters with clean tweezers.
4. Clean the skin around the wound with soap and water.

CLOSING AND COVERING
- Small, clean cuts can be closed with tape strips.
- Leave dirty wounds, bites and punctures open, covered with a clean dressing, so they can drain.
- Change dressings daily, or when wet or dirty.

SIGNS OF INFECTION
- Redness and swelling that spreads
- Increasing pain, warmth and pus
- Red streaks moving up from the wound, fever or chills: this is serious. Get to medical care urgently.

If infected: open the wound if it was closed, soak in warm clean water several times a day, keep it clean and covered.

BLISTERS
Cover hot spots early with tape. For large blisters, drain from the edge with a clean needle, leave the skin on, and pad around it.

WARNING: Animal bites can carry rabies. Wash any bite for 15 minutes with soap and water and get medical care as soon as possible.

SNAKEBITE

Most snakebites are not fatal, especially with calm, correct first aid and medical care.

DO
1. Move away from the snake. Do not try to catch or kill it; a photo from a distance is enough.
2. Keep the person calm and as still as possible.
3. Remove rings, watches and tight clothing from the bitten limb before swelling starts.
4. Keep the bitten limb still and at about heart level. Splint it if you can.
5. Mark the edge of swelling with a pen every 30 minutes, with the time.
6. Get to medical care as fast as safely possible. Carrying the person is better than letting them walk, if practical.

DO NOT
- Cut the wound or try to suck out venom
- Use a tourniquet
- Apply ice or heat
- Give alcohol or medicines like aspirin
- Use electric shock or "snakebite kits"

REGIONAL NOTE
In Australia, and for some snakes elsewhere (such as coral snakes, cobras and mambas), a pressure immobilization bandage is advised: wrap a wide elastic bandage firmly from the bite up the whole limb, then splint. Do not use this for North American rattlesnakes and other pit vipers, where it can increase tissue damage.

WARNING: Signs needing urgent help: trouble breathing or swallowing, drooping eyelids, bleeding gums, vomiting, fainting, rapidly spreading swelling.
